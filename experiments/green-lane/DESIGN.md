# Green Lane — design notes

An original two-player bluffing game designed *in* the DSL, as an exercise in
using the toolchain (checker → runtime → OpenSpiel adapter → solvers) as a
game-design feedback loop. This directory is an experiment, deliberately
outside `docs/games/` — the game is not (yet) corpus, so the registry/proof
pins don't apply. If it earns a place, it goes through
`docs/games/_candidates.md` like any other candidate.

## Design goal

- **New**: not a description of an existing game.
- **Strategically interesting in a measurable way**: the equilibrium should
  *require* mixed strategies (bluffing frequencies), and the tooling should be
  able to demonstrate that, not just assert it.
- **Small enough to solve**: a mini variant must be exactly solvable with
  vanilla CFR through the adapter, so "is it interesting?" gets a ground-truth
  answer (exploitability ≈ 0, interior action probabilities).
- **Pure-DSL**: no new grammar surface, no new stdlib primitives, no Python.
  Every construct used is already exercised by the corpus.

## The idea

A customs-inspection duel. Each player must ship their entire hand, one card
per turn, past the other player's limited inspections. High cards are
contraband; low cards are legal decoys. All hidden information is generated by
*concealed choices*, not by shuffling — there is no deal. (This also makes it
a nice stress case for the project's load-bearing claim: information sets
derive from zone visibility + move observations, and here the only hidden
things are choices routed through `HiddenPile` zones.)

Both starting hands are fixed, public knowledge. What the opponent doesn't
know is *which* cards you have already slipped through — waved-through
shipments land face down in your warehouse, so the composition of your
remaining hand blurs as the game runs. The inspector is card-counting a
distribution, not a certainty.

## Rules (full game)

Two players. P0 owns six clubs, P1 owns six spades: **2, 3, 4, 5, K, A** of
the suit. Card values: 2→1, 3→2, 4→3, 5→4, K→12, A→13. **K and A are
contraband**; the four low cards are legal goods. Each player starts with
**2 inspection tokens**. The fine is **4 points**.

The game is twelve smuggling rounds, alternating roles: on odd rounds P0 is
the merchant and P1 the inspector; on even rounds the roles swap. A smuggling
round:

1. **Ship.** The merchant secretly commits one card from hand, face down.
2. **Respond.** If the inspector has a token left, they choose **inspect** or
   **wave**. With no tokens, the shipment is waved automatically.
3. **Resolve.**
   - **Waved**: the card slides face down into the merchant's warehouse.
     Nobody learns what it was. It will score its value for the merchant.
   - **Inspected, legal** (2/3/4/5): false alarm. The card clears customs
     face up (still scores for the merchant), and the inspector pays the
     merchant the 4-point fine.
   - **Inspected, contraband** (K/A): busted. The card is seized — it scores
     for the *inspector* — and the merchant pays the inspector the 4-point
     fine.

After six rounds each, hands are empty and the game scores. Scoring is
zero-sum by construction: each player's final score is (their goods value +
**net** fines) minus (the opponent's goods value + net fines), where "goods
value" counts warehouse + cleared + seized cards. Because a fine is a
transfer (the payer loses what the receiver gains), a single fine of F moves
the two-player margin by 2F — with F = 4, a false alarm swings the margin by
8 and a caught ace by 34 (= 2·13 + 8). Highest score wins.

Because every legal card ends up scoring for the merchant no matter what
(waved → warehouse, inspected → cleared-with-a-fine), the strategic content is
exactly: **when do you run the K and the A, and when does the inspector spend
the two tokens**. Legal cards are interchangeable decoys — deliberately: the
strategy space stays legible (ship a decoy / ship K / ship A vs inspect /
wave), and the depth comes from timing, token economics, and belief-tracking
over the hidden warehouse.

### Why the payoffs are shaped this way

- False alarms *cost* the inspector (fine to the merchant): inspecting
  everything is not free, so the merchant can bait.
- A catch is a big swing (card value + fine to the inspector): the inspector's
  tokens are scarce but sharp, so the merchant can't just run contraband
  early and shrug.
- Tokens are public: when the inspector's tokens hit zero, deterrence
  evaporates and the merchant ships freely — so the inspector's *threat*
  has value, and spending the last token is a real decision.
- Contraband count (2) equals token count (2): the inspector can in principle
  catch everything, the merchant can in principle sneak everything.

Neither pure strategy survives: always-wave invites early contraband;
predictable inspection schedules get baited with decoys. The equilibrium has
to mix. That's the hypothesis the analysis tests.

## Mini variant (exactly solvable)

Same rules, hand of three (**2, 3, A** — one contraband), **1 token**,
fine 4, six rounds total. Small enough for vanilla CFR and exact
best-response through the adapter; used to verify the mixed-equilibrium claim
with ground truth before trusting the sampled analysis of the full game.

## Candidates considered and rejected

- **Compensation-GOPS** (sealed bids where the winner's bid card is paid to
  the loser): lowest implementation risk, but it's a twist on Goofspiel
  rather than a new game, and the 13!-order prize deck makes exact analysis
  impossible.
- **Declaration smuggling** (declare a value for the shipment; score what you
  declare unless challenged): richer, but the declaration multiplies the
  action space (~18 actions/turn) far past exact solvability, and it drifts
  toward Sheriff of Nottingham. The declaration layer is a natural *future*
  extension of Green Lane.
- **Concealed-play trick game** (follow suit or play face down): needs
  mid-trick hidden plays that the trick `round` form doesn't model; would
  push on new machinery rather than exercising what exists.

Family honesty: inspection games are a classic game-theory family, and
tabletop cousins exist (Sheriff of Nottingham's declare-and-inspect, the old
Pepys *Contraband*'s customs officer). The specific shape here — standard
deck, two players, fixed open hands with **zero deal randomness**, compulsory
total shipment, budgeted public inspections, hidden warehouse driving belief
decay, zero-sum fine/seizure economy — doesn't match any game I know of.

## Iteration outcome (post-evaluation)

The evaluation loop ran: baseline solves → an agent red-team → two rounds
of solved rule variants (see [REPORT.md](REPORT.md) §7–§9 and `variants/`).
The **recommended table ruleset is the V4 composition** of two variant
deltas onto the baseline:

- hand per player: **2, 3, 4, 5, 7, A** of one suit; **7 and A are
  contraband** (values 6 and 13 — a real "which contraband when" decision);
- **impound**: inspected-legal goods clear publicly but score nobody; the
  fine (raised to **5**) is the merchant's only compensation — so which
  decoy you feed to an inspection matters;
- everything else as below (2 tokens, hidden warehouse, zero-sum net-fine
  scoring).

Solved exactly at mini scale: fair to ±0.001, 71% of reached information
sets mixed, and the safest naive rule loses 10.8 points/game to a best
response (baseline: 6.0). The boldest structural direction — the V2 token
*bounty*, where a false alarm hands the spent token to the merchant and
couples a player's two roles into one game — is retained in `variants/` as
the expert/future track: it measurably rewires behaviour but carries a
first-mover edge (+0.66 after the V2b delay patch) that calls for
even-count match play or one more order-balancing iteration.

## Evaluation plan

Registered through the same general adapter as the corpus (path-based, seed
count 1 since the game has no chance), then:

1. **Sanity + complexity**: random-rollout stats (length, branching, returns
   spread), tree/infoset counts for the mini game.
2. **Ground truth (mini)**: vanilla CFR → exploitability trend → exact
   equilibrium behaviour at key information sets (is the merchant's round-1
   ship mixed? does the inspector mix inspect/wave?).
3. **Scaled evidence (full)**: outcome-sampling MCCFR; skill gradient by
   head-to-head (trained policy vs uniform random vs less-trained policy);
   mixture evidence from average-policy entropy at frequently reached
   information sets.
4. **Design feedback loop**: if the solver finds a degenerate optimum (e.g.
   never inspect, or always ship contraband on round 1), adjust fine/token
   parameters and re-measure.
